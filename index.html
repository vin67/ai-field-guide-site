What is an AI assistant?

Meet a very capable assistant – not an authority.

Step1.1

Part 1
Prompting
Time
2 min
Last reviewed
15 October 2026

Ask: “If the answer sounds polished, does that prove it is true?” Avoid a lengthy technical explanation or a debate about which assistant is best.

AI assistants such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Microsoft Copilot are built on large language models. They can draft, restructure, explain and spot gaps. They can also be confidently wrong, so you need to check anything that matters.

Mental model: Imagine a fast new starter who needs clear instructions and whose work you review.

It works from what you tell it, and may fill in missing information with guesses. Think of its first answer as a draft you improve by talking to it.

Same habits, any assistant: Each assistant, model and version words its answers differently, and the same prompt can give a different answer next time. Don’t expect your screen to match a colleague’s. The habits in this guide – clear prompts, deliberate follow-ups and checking the result – work in all of them.

Which assistant, and on whose terms?

The pattern

A system, not a model. The product wraps a language model in hidden instructions, tools and memory, so assess the whole system, not the model name. The model is a dependency the vendor upgrades underneath you, sometimes with little notice: a result you checked last month is not guaranteed today.

What is really being decided

Choosing an assistant is a platform decision with a core component you do not control. Its model, hidden instructions and features change on the vendor’s schedule. The organisation is really choosing whose pace of change, data terms and integrations it accepts – and whether people use one approved tool or several.

The forces

  • Capability against approval: new features often reach consumer plans before your enterprise agreement covers them.
  • Data terms: retention, use for training and region are set by the plan and contract, not by the product’s name.
  • Drift: the same product can answer differently after an upgrade you were not told about.
  • Shadow use: when the approved tool lags, people use others, and the organisation loses sight of its data.

Left open

  • Is one approved assistant enough, or should people choose by task?
  • Who tracks what changes when the vendor upgrades the model?
  • Would you rather know which unapproved tools staff use, or try to prevent them?
The model is one component, not the whole system

What you type into is a system, not just a model. Products typically wrap the language model in their own hidden instructions and add features such as web search, code execution, reading your files and memory; the mix differs by product. Two products built on the same model can therefore behave quite differently.

    Where does a conversation live, and who can read it?

    The pattern

    A stateless service with a client-held session. The model remembers nothing; the app resends the whole conversation with every message, like a stateless API where the client sends the session data each time. Step 3.3 shows what happens when that block gets too big.

    What is really being decided

    Because the conversation is resent every turn, the conversation is the data. Someone stores chat histories, for some period, readable by someone. Memory features go further and keep a running profile of each user. The decisions are whether chats that inform work are records, how long they are kept, and who may read them.

    The forces

    • Convenience against obligation: history and memory make the tool more useful and create data you must govern.
    • Records: a chat that shaped a decision may be a record under your organisation’s retention rules.
    • Cost: every message sends the whole history again as input, so long conversations cost more per turn.

    Left open

    • Are AI chat histories records in your organisation, and under which retention schedule?
    • Should memory features be on or off by default?
    • Who may read a colleague’s chat history, and in what circumstances?
    How a conversation carries context

    The model itself remembers nothing between messages. Each time you send one, the app packages the whole conversation so far – hidden instructions, your earlier messages and its earlier replies – into one long block of text and sends that to the model. A new chat starts without your earlier messages, which is why it forgets the last one (unless a memory feature adds notes back in).

    Better instructions produce more useful drafts.

    Useful prompts often include four ingredients. You do not need all four every time.

    PartQuestion to askExample
    GoalWhat do I want done?Review this entity definition.
    ContextWhat does it need to know?Conceptual model for business users.
    OutputWhat format should I get?A short problems table, then a rewrite.
    BoundariesWhat must it avoid?Do not invent missing facts.

    1. Try the weak version

    Start a new chat, paste this and read the result.

    Is this definition OK? Booking: The record in the system that tracks bookings.

    2. Try the improved version

    Paste this into another new chat, then compare the responses.

    Review the entity definition below. It's for a conceptual data model that business users will read. Give me a short table of problems (problem, why it matters, suggested fix), then a rewritten definition. Only use the information I've given you. If something is missing, list it as a question rather than making it up. Booking: The record in the system that tracks bookings.

    What to look for: Does the answer spot the circular definition, the focus on a system record, and the missing meaning of what gets booked?

    When does a prompt become an interface?

    The pattern

    An interface contract. Goal is the requirement, context the inputs, output the response format, boundaries the constraints – the same parts you already write for any interface.

    What is really being decided

    For a person reading the answer, a good prompt is enough. When an AI step feeds another system, the prompt becomes an integration contract: specify the output as a schema (for example JSON with named fields) and validate it before use – never parse free prose downstream. The real decision is which AI uses are conversations and which are integrations, because they need different controls.

    The forces

    • Flexibility against reliability: prose adapts to any question; structured output can be checked by a machine.
    • Ownership: a prompt inside an integration needs an owner, a version and tests, like any interface.
    • Vendor features: support for enforced output formats differs between products and changes.

    Left open

    • Which AI uses already feed another system without anyone treating them as an integration?
    • Who owns a prompt that sits inside an integration?
    • What should happen downstream when the output fails validation?
    A prompt is an interface contract

    The four parts map onto something you already write: goal is the requirement, context the inputs, output the response format and boundaries the constraints. The major vendors all publish prompting guides, and they give much the same advice: be specific, supply context and examples, say what format you want, and break big tasks into steps.

    Treat your next message as an edit instruction.

    Part 1
    Prompting

    Prompt the learner to challenge invented cardinalities and business rules. This is where “plausible” is different from “verified”.

    Return to the conversation with the stronger Booking prompt. Send each follow-up separately and inspect the result.

    Make the definition shorter – one sentence. What other entities would a Booking normally relate to? Describe each relationship in a sentence, like "A Customer makes zero or more Bookings."

    Critical thinking: Ask which suggested relationships are reasonable possibilities and which require actual business confirmation. Don't treat them as known facts.

    How do AI-suggested assumptions get into your designs?

    The pattern

    Anchoring. Each reply becomes context for the next, so an assumption made early survives every later edit unless someone challenges it. Steps 3.4 and 3.6 explain why it sticks.

    What is really being decided

    An assistant will happily propose cardinalities and business rules that sound right. The decision is how AI-suggested content is labelled and confirmed before it reaches a model, a standard or a decision: record it as an assumption, with an owner to confirm it, in the same place you log other design assumptions.

    The forces

    • Drafting speed against review capacity: the assistant drafts faster than subject experts can confirm.
    • Plausibility: suggestions that look like your own work are the hardest to question.
    • Provenance fades: once pasted into a model, nobody remembers which rules came from the assistant.

    Left open

    • Should AI-generated content in design artefacts be marked as such?
    • Who has the authority to confirm a business rule an assistant suggested?
    • How much unconfirmed content is acceptable in a draft that others will build on?
    Iterating works – and it anchors

    Follow-ups are effective. A 2023 study (Self-Refine) had models give feedback on their own drafts and then refine them; across seven tasks, results improved by about 20% (absolute) on average, although maths reasoning barely moved. The catch: each reply becomes context for the next. A 2025 study found that models often make assumptions in early turns and then rely on them too heavily – so an invented cardinality, say, can survive every later edit unless you challenge it directly.

    Whose standard does the assistant apply?

    The pattern

    Grounding: hand the assistant your source of truth. For definitions, that is your business glossary or data catalogue – the system of record for meaning.

    What is really being decided

    Without your standard, the assistant applies generic conventions from its training – plausible, but not yours – and every conversation invents its own. The decision is whether AI tools connect to the authoritative glossary or catalogue and answer only from it, and whether that source is good enough to be the reference.

    The forces

    • Connection exposes gaps: an assistant drawing on your glossary makes its gaps and contradictions visible to everyone.
    • The expensive part: curating the source, not connecting the tool.
    • Consistency against local meaning: different areas may define the same term differently, for good reasons.

    Left open

    • Is your glossary or catalogue authoritative enough to be the only source an assistant uses?
    • When two areas define the same term differently, which wins – and who decides?
    • Who maintains the source once AI tools depend on it?
    Grounding: give it your source of truth

    Without a standard to work from, the assistant applies whatever generic conventions it absorbed in training – plausible, but not yours. Supplying the authoritative source with the request is called grounding. At scale, systems fetch the relevant passages automatically before the model answers; a 2020 paper introduced the term retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) for this.

    When does a personal prompt become a shared asset?

    The pattern

    The rule of three from software refactoring: the third time you paste the same instructions, promote them to a reusable asset – a template or a skill – and put it under version control.

    What is really being decided

    A prompt a team relies on is configuration. The decision is where shared prompts live, who owns them, how they are versioned and tested, and at what point one becomes a skill or a feature of a product.

    The forces

    • Personal speed against shared reliability: my prompt works for me; ours has to work for everyone.
    • Sprawl: without a home, every person keeps a slightly different copy.
    • Portability: a prompt tuned for one model can behave differently on the next.

    Left open

    • Where would a library of approved prompts live, and who would curate it?
    • Should prompts used in decisions be reviewed like other standards?
    • What happens to a prompt when the model it was tuned for is retired?
    Prompts are assets: version and test them

    A prompt a team relies on is configuration, and deserves the same discipline: an owner, a version number, a short note of what changed, and a few test inputs whose results you check after every change. Step 2.5 does the testing part of this with a skill.

    What is a skill?

    A skill is a reusable set of instructions.

    Step2.1

    Part 2
    Reusable skills
    Time
    4 min
    Last reviewed
    15 October 2026

    Keep the discussion practical. Avoid frameworks, agent architecture or advanced tooling. If the learner’s plan has no skills feature, say so now and use the paste-in fallback – the learning outcome is the same.

    Instead of pasting the same long prompt each time, you can save the procedure as a skill. A skill is a plain-text Markdown file called SKILL.md, with instructions the assistant can apply when your request matches.

    Connection to Part 1: A skill is like your best four-part prompt written down for repeat use.

    A skill describes when it is useful and what steps to follow. It can include checks, common mistakes, a required output format and a final quality check. Skills are not a guarantee of accuracy.

    The SKILL.md layout follows an open format (the Agent Skills specification) that several assistants now read, but each product adds its own menus, limits and rules.

    Disclaimer – skills depend on your assistant, plan and version. Whether you can create, install or share skills depends on which assistant you use, your plan or licence, the app version, your region and age, and what your administrator has switched on. These features are often in preview and change quickly. Nothing in Part 2 requires a skills feature: where it is missing, use the paste-in fallback described in each step.

    Three ideas carry this part:

    1. A skill is your best prompt, written down. When you have typed the same prompt three times, write it down as a skill.
    2. The description decides when it runs. Write it from the user’s point of view, and include a “not for” line so it does not fire on the wrong work.
    3. A change only counts if the result improves. Adding rules can make an answer longer without making it better. Test every change against the same example before you share it.
    A catalogue of skills: who curates it?

    The pattern

    A service catalogue with lazy loading. Only each skill’s name and description are loaded until it is needed. The description is the routing key: it should say what the skill does and when to use it – write it like an API summary.

    What is really being decided

    Skills turn individual know-how into shared, reusable procedures. The decision is whether the organisation curates a catalogue of approved skills – naming, overlap, ownership, retirement – or lets them grow by accident, and whether to depend on the open format or on a vendor’s own.

    The forces

    • Reuse against sprawl: every useful skill invites three near-copies.
    • Routing conflicts: as the catalogue grows, overlapping descriptions compete for the same requests.
    • Portability: the open format is read by several assistants, but each adds its own rules and limits.
    • Availability: skills features depend on plan, region and administrator settings.

    Left open

    • Who decides which skills are approved and shared?
    • How would you stop two skills competing for the same requests?
    • Is an open format worth giving up a vendor’s richer features?
    Progressive disclosure: why skills scale

    An assistant can have many skills installed without filling its context window, because it does not read them all. Under the open Agent Skills format, only each skill’s name and description are loaded at the start. The full instructions are loaded only when the skill is activated, and any extra files – scripts or reference documents – only when they are needed. The specification calls this progressive disclosure.

    Skills are a supply chain: what is your intake process?

    The pattern

    Third-party code. A skill steers what the assistant does, and some include scripts it can run. Apply the controls you use for third-party code: trusted sources only, read before installing, approved skills under version control, and the least access needed.

    What is really being decided

    Installing a skill means accepting instructions, and possibly code, into your environment. The decision is the intake process: who may publish, who reviews, what a review checks, and how an approved skill is updated or withdrawn.

    Which layer should each instruction live in?

    The pattern

    Layered configuration – defaults, environment, overrides. Instructions reach the model from the vendor, the organisation, the project, skills and your message. Put stable rules in the highest layer you control, task detail in the lowest, and keep each rule in exactly one place so you know which version won.

    What is really being decided

    Conflicts between layers are resolved in ways you cannot always see. The decision is which rules the organisation sets once for everyone, which belong to a project or a skill, and which are left to each person.

    The forces

    • Central control against local flexibility: rules set for everyone rarely fit every team.
    • Visibility: the vendor’s own top layer is hidden from you.
    • Duplication: the same rule in two layers will eventually disagree with itself.

    Most people who use AI assistants have watched a chat go off the rails, or met the assistant that agrees, apologises and then repeats the mistake. These are not random glitches.     Five ideas

    1. It reads tokens, not words It sees chunks of words, so counting letters and words is unreliable.
    2. Its working memory is finite Each reply sees only what fits in the window, and the oldest material goes first.

    Tokens are your unit of cost and capacity: who plans for them?

    The pattern

    An encoding layer. The model never sees your raw data, only an encoded form of it, so tasks that depend on the raw form are unreliable. Capacity planning for an AI feature is token budgeting: tokens per transaction – prompt, attached documents and reply – times the volume, as you would size bandwidth or storage.

    What is really being decided

    Tokens set the price, the speed and the limits. The decisions are how an AI feature is sized and costed before launch, who carries the bill as use grows, and what happens at the vendor’s rate limit. The same encoding sits under meaning search: when someone proposes vector search or RAG, someone has to decide how “close enough” is set and tested.

    The forces

    • Cost follows use: per-token pricing scales with volume and with the size of every document attached.
    • Ceilings you do not set: rate limits are capacity limits chosen by the vendor.
    • Language: the same content can take up to 15 times as many tokens in some languages – a cost and fairness question for multilingual services.
    • Prices move: in both directions, at the vendor’s discretion.

    Tokens are the unit of cost, speed and limits

    Tokens are more than a curiosity. Paid AI services price usage per million tokens and set rate limits in tokens per minute, and each model can handle only so many tokens at once – its context window. Long replies take longer because the model generates one token at a time, each depending on those before it. The same text can also take very different numbers of tokens in different languages: a 2023 study found differences of up to 15 times.

    From tokens to meaning: how “close enough” is found

    Each token has a number, and the model only ever works with those numbers – a small model such as GPT-2 has a vocabulary of about 50,000 of them. Each number is then turned into a long list of numbers called an embedding (768 per token in that small model). Think of the list as an address in a “meaning space”. Training moves words that are used in similar ways close together, so “board”, “committee” and “panel” end up as neighbours. In older word-embedding models even directions carried meaning: the classic example is king − man + woman ≈ queen.

    The biggest whoppers

    Designing with a component that can be confidently wrong

    The pattern

    A probabilistic component inside a deterministic system. Same input, possibly different output, and no built-in sense of which statements are facts. Its knowledge is a lossy compression of its training data: it holds no records, runs no queries and has no reliable record of what it does not know.

    What is really being decided

    Never design on the assumption that the model gives the same answer twice, or that it knows a fact. The decisions are where verification sits and what kind: provenance kept with AI-generated content (the source it used, the model, the date); deterministic controls where repeatability matters – a fixed script, a schema check, a lookup in the system of record; and human review where an error would be costly. Facts that matter come from a system of record, not the model’s memory.

    The forces

    • Accuracy hides confident errors: in OpenAI’s comparison, the older model scored slightly higher on accuracy and was wrong about three times as often.
    • “I don’t know” reads as weakness: users and buyers may prefer the model that always answers.
    • Provenance is not kept by default: most tools drop the source, model and date as soon as text is copied.

    Left open

    • When assessing an AI product, would you trade some accuracy for fewer confident errors?
    • Should AI-generated content carry provenance in your systems – and who would read it?

    Why hallucination is built in – and what reduces it

    A 2025 research paper argues that hallucinations persist partly because training and evaluation reward guessing over admitting uncertainty – like a student on a hard exam who guesses rather than leaving the answer blank, because guessing scores better. In the paper’s terms, a model optimised to score well on such tests learns to guess when unsure.

    General advice, beyond the paper: setting temperature to zero does not fix this, because it only makes the model pick its most likely answer, which can still be wrong. What reduces the risk is supplying the source (grounding), asking for citations you then check, and making it acceptable to answer “I don’t know”.

    Temperature 0 is not a repeat button

    At temperature 0 the model should always pick the top word and give the same answer every time. On a shared service it often does not. Researchers at Thinking Machines Lab sent the prompt “Tell me about Richard Feynman” 1,000 times at temperature 0 and got 80 different answers. All matched for the first 102 tokens; then 992 said Feynman was born in “Queens, New York” and 8 said “New York City”.

    The cause is batching: the server groups your request with other people’s, and the size of that group – which depends on how busy the server is – changes the arithmetic slightly. It can be fixed, at some cost in speed. A model running only for you, such as Transformer Explainer in your browser, repeats at low temperature because nothing is batched.

    Separation of duties when the reviewer wants to please

    The pattern

    The wrong optimisation target: a system rewarded for approval will give you approval. Its behaviour was tuned, not built from rules you can inspect, so treat it as a component with known tendencies. Separation of duties applies.

    What is really being decided

    How AI is used in review and assurance. Do not let the assistant author and review the same work in one chat, and do not signal the answer you want; for a review, give it the role of critic in a fresh chat, with the criteria written down. The harder decision is whether an AI review can ever count as a control.

    The forces

    • Cheap and fast: AI review is tempting as a substitute for the human kind.
    • Varies by model: how agreeable a model is can change between versions.
    • Not only machines: organisations reward agreement in people too; AI can amplify an existing bias.

    Left open

    Sycophancy is measurable

    Researchers call this sycophancy. A 2023 study found it in all five leading AI assistants of the time that it tested: they admitted mistakes they had not made when challenged, and changed answers to agree with a user’s stated beliefs, even weakly expressed ones. The authors suggest it is likely driven in part by human preference judgements: both people and the models trained on their ratings preferred convincingly written sycophantic answers over correct ones a non-negligible fraction of the time.

    Read more

    • Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models (arXiv, 2023)
    Where the helpfulness comes from

    Training happens in two stages. In pre-training, the model plays “predict the next word” across a vast amount of text while billions of internal numbers – its parameters – are adjusted. The result has absorbed a great many patterns but can only continue text. In post-training, example conversations and human and automated feedback turn it into a helpful assistant. That feedback is also one reason it tends to agree with you.

    The model does not learn from your chat while you talk to it: its parameters do not change. Memory features store notes separately and add them back into the context. Whether conversations are used for future training depends on the product’s settings and your organisation’s agreement – check the guidance for your approved tool rather than assuming.

    Questions people ask

    Does it understand, or is it just autocomplete?

    Both framings miss something. It is trained only to predict the next token, yet research that traces what happens inside a model (step 3.10) shows it builds internal concepts, plans ahead and combines facts in steps. What matters for architects is that none of that makes it reliable on its own – which is why the controls in the decision grid exist.

    What are reasoning or thinking models?

    Models trained to write out intermediate working before the final answer, which gives them more computation per answer and usually better results on hard problems. The written working is still generated text: it is not guaranteed to be what actually happened inside.

    Doesn’t retrieval (RAG) fix hallucination?

    It reduces it by putting authoritative text into the context, but the model can still misread, mix or ignore what it retrieved, and retrieval can fetch the wrong thing. It moves the problem to the quality of the source and the retrieval.

    OpenAI’s own research says accuracy will never reach 100%, because some questions cannot be answered, but that models can learn to say “I don’t know”. Expect fewer confident errors, not none.

    Where should your organisation’s knowledge live?

    The pattern

    Separate knowledge from processing, as you separate data from application logic. RAG is a read-only integration: the model stays the same, and each request puts different text in its context window (step 3.3). When facts change, you change data, not the model.

    What is really being decided

    Not “RAG or not”, but which knowledge the organisation will keep as owned, current, machine-readable sources – and which questions should be answered by a system directly instead. An assistant can be no better than the content it is handed, so most of the work behind it is information management, not AI.

    The forces

    • Freshness: change a document and the next answer changes. Teaching facts by fine-tuning means training again for every change.
    • Traceability: retrieved answers can cite their source; a model’s memory cannot.
    • Where the cost goes: away from training and into ingestion, indexing and keeping content current – a running cost, not a project cost.
    • Accountability: content owners become answerable for AI answers they never see.

    Left open

    • When an answer is wrong, who owns it: the content owner, the platform team or the vendor?
    • Should “ready for AI” be a standard your content has to meet, and who would enforce it?
    • Which knowledge would you never let an assistant answer from, however good the retrieval?
    Where RAG came from, and what it is not

    The term comes from a 2020 research paper that combined a language model with a search over a document collection, so answers could draw on text the model was never trained on – and could be updated by changing the documents rather than the model.

    It is often confused with fine-tuning, which trains the model further on your examples and changes its parameters. Fine-tuning is good at teaching a style or a format; it is a poor way to add facts that change, because every change means training again, and the model still cannot point to where a fact came from. RAG leaves the model alone and changes its input, so facts can be updated by updating documents, and answers can cite them.

    What is an instruction to the model worth as a control?

    The pattern

    Grounding is just context. “Answer only from the sources” is a soft control: a policy written down, with nothing to enforce it. Everything from Part 3 still applies – a long paste spreads attention thin, and instructions can be outweighed.

    What is really being decided

    Whether a soft control is enough for this use case, and which hard control sits after generation where it is not.     • Cost of checking against cost of error: an automatic quote check costs little; a person checking every answer costs a lot – and a wrong answer may cost more.

    Real document collections are far too big to paste. So a RAG system splits each document into short passages – often called chunks – and searches them for every question. Only the best few go to the model. If the right passage is not among them, the model never sees it.

    Chunking strategies: where to cut

    Every RAG system has to decide where one passage ends and the next begins. The cut decides what search can find: split a rule from its exception and no single passage holds the whole answer. The common strategies, from simplest to most elaborate (checked 30 October 2026):

    StrategyHow it cutsTrade-off

    How much overlap? The sources disagree, which is itself the lesson. Microsoft suggests starting at 512 tokens with 25% overlap (128 tokens); Weaviate suggests 10–20%; OpenAI’s file search defaults to 800-token passages with 400 tokens of overlap; Chroma’s tests found 200-token passages with no overlap performed consistently well. Overlap helped small passages in Chroma’s tests, but it also returns the same text twice and spends tokens on it.

    The evidence. Anthropic reported that adding a line of context to each passage cut failed retrievals by 35%; with keyword search added, 49%; with reranking as well, 67%. Chroma found the choice of strategy alone moved recall by up to 9 percentage points. No strategy wins on every collection: the documents, the kind of question and the embedding model all change the answer.

    What has changed. Models now read hundreds of thousands of tokens, so a small collection may not need chunking at all: Anthropic suggests that below about 200,000 tokens (roughly 500 pages) you put the whole thing in the prompt. Long inputs still degrade, though (step 3.4), so for large collections the cut still matters. And the platforms now do more of it for you – Azure AI Search, for example, can chunk by layout and meaning as part of indexing.

    In practice: start with recursive or page-level cuts of a few hundred tokens, keep the heading and page with each passage, and change one thing at a time against a test set of real questions (see “Hybrid search and reranking” below).

    Build or buy the ingestion pipeline?

    The pattern

    Ingestion is an ETL pipeline: extract text from documents, transform it into clean passages with metadata, load it into an index. Retrieval is a search product built on top. Chunk size is a granularity decision, like partitioning data: too small and a rule loses its context; too large and every passage carries noise and costs more tokens.

    What is really being decided

    Every document has to become clean passages with metadata before search can work, and documents mix text, scans, tables, charts and images. The extraction step can be built from open-source Python libraries, bought as a vendor’s document AI service, or handed to a model that reads pages directly – and in practice often all three, by document type. This guide does not make that choice for you; it lays out what the choice involves.

    The forces

    • Cost: building costs engineering time and compute; a service charges per page; a model charges per token, which adds up across a large collection. Building can be cheaper to run and dearer to own.
    • Control and change: your own pipeline can be tuned and inspected, and you have to keep up with a field that changes monthly. A vendor improves – and changes – on its own schedule.
    • Where the data goes: a library runs inside your environment; a service or model sends documents to a vendor, in a region, under its terms.
    • Licences: open source is not one licence. Some PDF libraries are AGPL or GPL, which matters if you distribute or offer the software as a service.
    • Skills: someone has to run, monitor and fix whatever you choose – including at 2 am when a new document format breaks it.
    • Switching cost: change the extractor or the embedding model and the whole collection is processed again. Keeping the extracted output in your own open format (for example Markdown or JSON with page references) keeps the extractor replaceable.
    • Proof: only a test set of your own hard documents – scanned forms, multi-column pages, tables across pages – shows which option works for you. Vendor benchmarks use their documents, not yours.

    Left open

    • Is document extraction a shared platform capability, or does each project choose its own?
    • Where should that decision be made, and how often revisited, in a field that changes this fast?
    • Which documents may leave your environment to be processed, and who decides?
    • Would you rather own the pipeline and its upkeep, or accept a vendor’s pace of change?
    • Does the index live in a database you already run, or in a dedicated search service? (The options are in “Where the index lives” below.)
    Getting text, tables and images out of documents

    Text layer or picture of text. A PDF made from a word processor has a text layer that can be read directly. A scanned PDF is an image of text and needs optical character recognition (OCR). Many collections hold both, and one file can mix them.

    Layout and reading order. A PDF stores characters at positions on a page, not paragraphs. Columns, headers, footers, footnotes and sidebars have to be put back in reading order, or one passage can splice two columns together. Layout-aware tools first detect headings, paragraphs, lists, tables and figures.

    Tables. Flattened into text, a table loses which number belongs to which row and column. Common strategies: keep each table whole, as Markdown or HTML, with its caption and headings; index a short summary for search and hand the full table to the model; for long tables, store rows with their column headings repeated; and for numbers that matter, query the system the table came from rather than the PDF.

    Images and charts. Text extraction skips them. The options: leave them out, as a deliberate decision; read the text inside them with OCR; ask a vision model to describe each figure and index the description, linked to the image; or index the page images themselves with a vision-language model, so a page can be found by how it looks as well as what it says (the ColPali approach, 2024).

    Chunking. Once the text is out, it is cut into passages – see “Chunking strategies: where to cut” above.

    Metadata. Store the document, section, page, version, effective date, owner and access rules with every passage, so results can be filtered before the model sees them.

    Three kinds of tool (examples, checked 25 October 2026): open-source Python libraries, such as pypdf or pdfplumber for text layers, Tesseract for OCR, and Docling or Unstructured for layout, tables and figures; document AI services from cloud vendors, such as Azure AI Document Intelligence, Amazon Textract and Google Document AI; and multimodal models such as Claude, GPT and Gemini, which accept PDFs and read text, tables and charts from the page images.

    Hybrid search and reranking

    Many production systems combine both kinds of matching, called hybrid search. Keyword search is strong on exact terms – unit codes, policy numbers, names – where meaning search can blur; meaning search is strong on paraphrases. A second model, a reranker, often re-sorts the top results before they reach the language model.

    Relevance is measured the way search teams measure it: a list of real questions, the passage each should find, and how often that passage comes back in the top few results. The measurement is rerun after every change to the content, the chunking or the model.

    • Duplication against speed: every team building its own is fast at first and expensive to run, secure and test later.
    • Copies of your content: every index is another copy of your content, in another place, under another set of terms.

    Left open

    • Should grounding be one shared capability or a choice each project makes?
    • How many copies of your content in vendors’ indexes are acceptable, and who keeps count?
    • What would make you move from a platform’s built-in AI to your own layer – or back?
    Testing a RAG system

    A RAG system has two parts that fail differently, so test them separately. Retrieval: for each question in a test set, did the passage that holds the answer come back in the top few? Generation: is every statement supported by the retrieved passages (faithfulness), and does the answer address the question?

    Open-source frameworks such as Ragas automate some of these checks by using a second model as the judge – which, as step 2.5 noted, needs checking too. Rerun the set whenever the content, the chunking, the template or the model changes.

    Two minutes on the three ideas, then open the discussion. The room answers first: read two or three answers aloud before adding your own. Anything that needs more than 30 seconds goes on the parking lot, read back before the close.

    Three ideas carry this part:

    1. RAG changes the context, not the model. It hands the model pages; the answer is only as good as the pages it gets.
    2. Nearest is not the same as relevant. Ask how “close enough” is decided, and what happens when nothing is close.
    3. The index is inside your trust boundary. Old versions, unofficial sources, hidden instructions and missing permissions all flow straight into answers.

    • The AI built into the platforms you already use often searches your content before it answers. What does it retrieve from, and who controls that?
    • Where would a wrong citation do the most damage?
    • What should an assistant refuse to answer, even with good sources?

    The pattern

    Documents are for explanation; systems of record are for facts. Use retrieval for the first and a query for the second.

    What is really being decided

    Not which tool, but which capabilities have to exist first: content that is owned, current and fit to be retrieved; an ingestion pipeline someone runs; a test set that is rerun after every change; and decision rights – who admits sources, who owns the template, who answers for a wrong answer. Those are the questions this part has left open on purpose.

    The forces

    • Content estate: the quality ceiling of every assistant you deploy.
    • Pace of change: models, extraction tools and vendor features move monthly; your governance moves quarterly at best.
    • Where AI already is: the platforms you use are adding retrieval whether or not you plan for it.

    Left open

    • Which of the questions left open in this part matters most for your area – and who should answer it?
    • Where should these decisions be made: in each project, in an architecture forum, or in a platform team?
    • What would you want in place before the next AI feature switches on in a platform you already use?
    When documents are not enough: GraphRAG and structured truth

    Plain RAG retrieves passages, so it struggles with questions that need relationships across many documents – “which applications depend on systems that retire next year?” Approaches such as GraphRAG, published by Microsoft researchers in 2024, first build a knowledge graph from the documents and then retrieve from the graph.

    Where an organisation already keeps structured truth – a business glossary, a canonical data model, a capability map, an application inventory – connecting the assistant to it as a tool is often more reliable than extracting the same facts back out of documents.
